%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\chapter{A Term Assignment for LFD}\label{ch:pi-fd}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

In this section, we introduce a term assignment system for $\LFD$: $\piFD$.
We present its syntax, typing, and operational semantics, as well as the
interpretation of the logical connectives in the process calculus setting.
A demonstration of how to program using $\piFD$ is given in
Section \ref{ch:programming-with-sessions}. A detailed presentation of
$\piFD$'s metatheory is postponed to the third part of this thesis.

\section{Syntax and Typing}
The language of types is given by Definition \ref{def:pi-fd:types};
the constructs themselves will be explained in detail in Section \ref{ch:pi-fd:op-semantics}.
Since we do not consider polymorphism, we will drop propositional variables,
as opposed to the propositions of $\LFD$.

\begin{definition}[Types of $\piFD$] \label{def:pi-fd:types}
  \begin{alignat*}{4}
    & A, B \; &&&\Coloneqq &\; A \oplus B  &\qquad \tag*{\textit{selection}} & \\
    &         &&&\mid      &\; A \with B   &\qquad \tag*{\textit{choice}   } & \\
    &         &&&\mid      &\; A \otimes B &\qquad \tag*{\textit{output}   } & \\
    &         &&&\mid      &\; A \parr B   &\qquad \tag*{\textit{input}    } & \\
    &         &&&\mid      &\; 1           &\qquad \tag*{\textit{close}    } & \\
    &         &&&\mid      &\; \bot        &\qquad \tag*{\textit{wait}     } &
  \end{alignat*}
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Syntax of $\piFD$] \label{def:pi-fd:syntax}
  \phantom{foo}
  \vspace{-1.5em}

  \begin{minipage}[t]{0.40\textwidth}
    \begin{flalign*}
      P, Q \; &\Coloneq &\!\!\!\!\!&                      & \textbf{Process} \\
              &         &\!\!\!\!\!& \link{M}{M}          & \textit{link} \\
              &\;\;\mid &\!\!\!\!\!& \parcomp{x}{y}{P}{Q} & \textit{cut} \\
              &\;\;\mid &\!\!\!\!\!& P \seq xS            & \textit{sequencing} \\
              &\;\;\mid &\!\!\!\!\!& \Lambda              & \textit{nil process} \\
      M \;    &\Coloneq &\!\!\!\!\!&                      & \textbf{Message} \\
              &         &\!\!\!\!\!& \future{x}           & \textit{future} \\
              &\;\;\mid &\!\!\!\!\!& S                    & \textit{statement}
    \end{flalign*}
  \end{minipage}
  \hfill
  \begin{minipage}[t]{0.40\textwidth}
    \begin{flalign*}
      S \; &\Coloneq &\!\!\!\!\!&                    & \textbf{Statement} \\
           &         &\!\!\!\!\!& \slselect{x.P}     & \textit{selection} \\
           &\;\;\mid &\!\!\!\!\!& \srselect{x.P}     & \textit{selection} \\
           &\;\;\mid &\!\!\!\!\!& \schoice{x.P}{x.P} & \textit{choice} \\
           &\;\;\mid &\!\!\!\!\!& \ssend{x.P}{x.P}   & \textit{output} \\
           &\;\;\mid &\!\!\!\!\!& \sreceive{x}{x}{P} & \textit{input} \\
           &\;\;\mid &\!\!\!\!\!& \sclose            & \textit{close} \\
           &\;\;\mid &\!\!\!\!\!& \swait{P}          & \textit{wait}
    \end{flalign*}
  \end{minipage}
\end{definition}

The syntax of $\piFD$ differentiates between \emph{processes}, \emph{statements}
and \emph{messages}. The last two syntactic categories are not necessary,
however they allow the operational semantics to be written more concisely.
Similarly, we add the nil process $\Lambda$ to allow for closed processes,
at the cost of losing consistency.

\begin{definition}[Contexts] \label{def:pi-fd:contexts}
  The contexts of $\piFD$ are inductively defined by the grammar
  \[
    \Gamma \Coloneqq \varnothing \mid \Gamma, x : A
  \]
  and are treated as unordered lists. The subjects of the declarations in
  a context are pairwise distinct. We refer to the set of all subjects of
  a context $\Gamma$ as the \emph{domain} $\dom(\Gamma)$.
\end{definition}

Since we are dealing with a linear typing discipline, we define the relation
$\Gamma = \Delta_1 \circ \Delta_2$, which describes how a context may be split
into two disjoint contexts\footnote{In a named representation with unordered
contexts this merely amounts to a linear union. However, in our formalization,
contexts are lists ordered based on their de Bruijn index. Many proofs thus
make use of an induction on the derivation of a context split, which is why
we did not want to omit their definition.}.

\begin{definition}[Context split] \label{def:pi-fd:ctx-split}
  The relation $\Gamma = \Delta_1 \circ \Delta_2$ is inductively defined by the
  following rules, where we presuppose that $\dom(\Delta_1) \cap \dom(\Delta_2) = \varnothing$.
  \begin{center}
    \begin{prooftree}
      \hypo{}
      \infer1[\textsc{split-e}]{\varnothing = \varnothing \circ \varnothing}
    \end{prooftree}

    \bigskip

    \begin{prooftree}
      \hypo{\Gamma = \Delta_1 \circ \Delta_2}
      \infer1[\textsc{split-l}]{\Gamma, x : A = (\Delta_1, x : A) \circ \Delta_2}
    \end{prooftree}
    \hfill
    \begin{prooftree}
      \hypo{\Gamma = \Delta_1 \circ \Delta_2}
      \infer1[\textsc{split-r}]{\Gamma, x : A = \Delta_1 \circ (\Delta_2, x : A)}
    \end{prooftree}
  \end{center}
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Typing rules of $\piFD$] \label{def:pi-fd:typing}
  \phantom{Foo}
  \begin{center}
    \begin{flushright}
      Processes \framebox{$\Gamma \vdash P$}
    \end{flushright}

    \begin{prooftree}
      \hypo{\Gamma \vdash M_1 : A}
      \hypo{\Delta \vdash M_2 : A^\bot}
      \infer2[\textsc{t-ax}]{\Gamma \circ \Delta \vdash \link{M_1}{M_2}}
    \end{prooftree}

    \bigskip

    \begin{prooftree}
      \hypo{\Gamma, x : A \vdash P}
      \hypo{\Delta, y : A^\bot \vdash Q}
      \infer2[\textsc{t-cut}]{\Gamma \circ \Delta \vdash \parcomp{x}{y}{P}{Q}}
    \end{prooftree}
    \hfill
    \begin{prooftree}
      \hypo{\Gamma, x : A \vdash P}
      \hypo{\Delta \vdash S : A}
      \infer2[\textsc{t-seq}]{\Gamma \circ \Delta \vdash P \seq xS}
    \end{prooftree}

    \bigskip

    \begin{prooftree}
      \hypo{}
      \infer1[\textsc{t-nil}]{\vdash \Lambda}
    \end{prooftree}

    \begin{flushright}
      Messages \framebox{$\Gamma \vdash M : A$} and
      statements \framebox{$\Gamma \vdash S : A$}
    \end{flushright}

    \begin{prooftree}[center=false]
      \hypo{}
      \infer1[\textsc{t-id}]{x : A \vdash x : A}
    \end{prooftree}

    \bigskip

    \begin{prooftree}
      \hypo{\Gamma, z : A^\bot \vdash Q}
      \infer1[\textsc{t-$\oplus_1$}]{\Gamma \vdash \slselect{z.Q} : A \oplus B}
    \end{prooftree}
    \qquad
    \begin{prooftree}
      \hypo{\Gamma, z : B^\bot \vdash Q}
      \infer1[\textsc{t-$\oplus_2$}]{\Gamma \vdash \srselect{z.Q} : A \oplus B}
    \end{prooftree}

    \bigskip

    \begin{prooftree}
      \hypo{\Gamma, z_1 : A^\bot \vdash Q_1}
      \hypo{\Gamma, z_2 : B^\bot \vdash Q_2}
      \infer2[\textsc{t-$\with$}]{\Gamma \vdash \schoice{z_1.Q_1}{z_2.Q_2} : A \with B}
    \end{prooftree}

    \bigskip

    \begin{prooftree}
      \hypo{\Gamma, v : A^\bot \vdash Q}
      \hypo{\Delta, z : B^\bot \vdash R}
      \infer2[\textsc{t-$\otimes$}]{\Gamma \circ \Delta \vdash \ssend{v.Q}{z.R} : A \otimes B}
    \end{prooftree}
    \qquad
    \begin{prooftree}
      \hypo{\Gamma, v : A^\bot, z : B^\bot \vdash Q}
      \infer1[\textsc{t-$\parr$}]{\Gamma \vdash \sreceive{v}{z}{Q} : A \parr B}
    \end{prooftree}

    \bigskip

    \begin{prooftree}[center=false]
      \hypo{}
      \infer1[\textsc{t-$1$}]{\vdash \sclose{} : 1}
    \end{prooftree}
    \qquad
    \begin{prooftree}[center=false]
      \hypo{\Gamma \vdash Q}
      \infer1[\textsc{t-$\bot$}]{\Gamma \vdash \swait{Q} : \bot}
    \end{prooftree}
  \end{center}
\end{definition}

The processes of $\piFD$ are the term assignments for $\LFD$ derivations.
A link consisting of two futures corresponds to an axiom of $\LFD$.
Every elimination rule is represented by a sequencing, i.e. applying
multiple elimination rules in succession corresponds to a sequence of communication
statements. For the elimination rule of $\otimes$, we obtain the following
term decoration:

\begin{center}
  \begin{prooftree}
    \hypo{\Gamma_1, x : A \otimes B \vdash P}
    \hypo{\Gamma_2, v : A^\bot \vdash Q}
    \hypo{\Gamma_3, z : B^\bot \vdash R}
    \infer2[\textsc{t-$\otimes$}]{\Gamma_2 \circ \Gamma_3 \vdash \ssend{v.Q}{z.R} : A \otimes B}
    \infer2[\textsc{t-seq}]{\Gamma_1 \circ \Gamma_2 \circ \Gamma_3 \vdash P \seq x\ssend{v.Q}{z.R}}
  \end{prooftree}
\end{center}

The statements of $\piFD$ exactly correspond to the secondary premises
of every elimination rule in $\LFD$. Having them live in their own syntactic
category allows the permutation of logical rules to be expressed by a substitution
of messages for futures: a process $P \seq xS$ \emph{fulfills} the future
$x$ with the message $S$ by substituting $S$ for $x$.

However, adding these new syntactic categories comes at the cost of slightly loosening
the correspondence between processes and derivations: The logical redex

\begin{center}
  \begin{prooftree}
    \hypo{1, \bot \vdash}
    \infer1[$1$]{\bot \vdash}
    \hypo{\Gamma \vdash}
    \infer2[$\bot$]{\Gamma \vdash}
  \end{prooftree}
\end{center}
possesses four different term assignments
\begin{gather*}
  \link{x}{y} \seq x\sclose \seq y\swait{P} \\
  \link{\sclose}{y} \seq y\swait{P} \\
  \link{x}{\swait{P}} \seq x\sclose \\
  \link{\sclose}{\swait{P}}
\end{gather*}
given $\Gamma \vdash P$ for some $\Gamma$. They correspond to no, one, or both
futures representing a channel being fulfilled. Once both sides are fulfilled,
we obtain a logical redex.

\begin{definition}[Logical redexes in $\piFD$]
  A link is a logical redex, if it is of one of the following forms.
  \begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
    \item $\link{\ssend{v.P}{z.Q}}{\sreceive{a}{b}{R}}$
    \item $\link{\slselect{z.P}}{\schoice{v.Q}{w.R}}$
    \item $\link{\srselect{z.P}}{\schoice{v.Q}{w.R}}$
    \item $\link{\sclose}{\swait{P}}$
  \end{enumerate}
\end{definition}

As in $\LFD$, we use a congruence relation to factor out syntactic differences
of processes that are not significant for their behavior, such as the orientation
of a link.

\section{Structural Congruence}
The structural proof conversions of $\LFD$ yield the rules of the \emph{structural congruence}
$\equiv$ of $\piFD$.

\begin{definition}[Structural congruence] \label{def:pi-fd:struct-cong}
  Structural congruence $\equiv$ is defined as the least congruence relation
  that is closed under rules
  \begin{align*}
    \link{x}{y} &\equiv \link{y}{x} \tag{\textsc{c-link}} \\
    \parcomp{x}{y}{P}{Q} &\equiv \parcomp{y}{x}{Q}{P} \tag{\textsc{c-comm}} \\
    \parcomp{v}{z}{\parcomp{x}{y}{P}{Q}}{R}
      &\equiv \parcomp{x}{y}{P}{\parcomp{v}{z}{Q}{R}} \tag{\textsc{c-assoc}}
  \end{align*}
  where for \textsc{c-assoc} we presuppose that $v \notin \fv(P)$ and $y \notin \fv(R)$.
\end{definition}

\textsc{c-comm} and \textsc{c-assoc} correspond
to the conversions \textsc{CComm} and \textsc{CAssoc}, respectively.
Together, they allow us to view an abstract syntax tree built from nested \textsc{cut}s
as a flat multiset of processes.

The structural proof conversion \textsc{LComm} is captured by the axiom
\textsc{c-link}, enabling channel bidirectionality.

\section{Free Variables and Substitution}
In order to present the interesting conversions of process reductions, we
must first deal with the notions of free variables and substitution. We first
define the set of free variables of processes, statements and terms.

\begin{definition}[Free variables] \label{def:pi-fd:free-vars}
  The set of free variables, written $\fv(P)$ ($\fv(M)$) is recursively defined
  as follows:
  {\allowdisplaybreaks
  \begin{align*}
    \fv(\link{M_1}{M_2}) &\coloneq \fv(M_1) \cup \fv(M_2) \\
    \fv(\parcomp{x}{y}{P}{Q}) &\coloneq (\fv(P) \cup \fv(Q)) \setminus \{x, y\} \\
    \fv(P \seq xS) &\coloneq (\fv(P) \setminus x) \cup \fv(S) \\
    \fv(\Lambda) &\coloneq \varnothing \\
    \fv(x) &\coloneq \{x\} \\
    \fv(\slselect{x.P}) &\coloneq \fv(P) \setminus x \\
    \fv(\srselect{x.P}) &\coloneq \fv(P) \setminus x \\
    \fv(\schoice{x.P}{y.Q}) &\coloneq (\fv(P) \setminus x) \cup (\fv(Q) \setminus y) \\
    \fv(\ssend{x.P}{y.Q}) &\coloneq (\fv(P) \setminus x) \cup (\fv(Q) \setminus y) \\
    \fv(\sreceive{x}{y}{P}) &\coloneq \fv(P) \setminus \{x, y\} \\
    \fv(\sclose) &\coloneq \varnothing \\
    \fv(\swait{P}) &\coloneq \fv(P)
  \end{align*}
  }
  We say that a process is \emph{closed}, if its set of free variables is empty.
\end{definition}

A consequence of linear typing is that the typing contexts and
the set of free variables for well-typed processes coincide.

\begin{lemma}[\rocqicon~Domains are strict] \label{lemma:strict-domains}
  If $\Gamma \vdash P$ ($\Gamma \vdash M$), then $\dom(\Gamma) = \fv(P)$ ($\fv(M)$).
\end{lemma}

Lemma \ref{lemma:strict-domains} tells us that the channels a process
may externally communicate along are exactly the axioms that are yet to be the subject
of a communication statement.

The second notion, substitution, is the primary operation enabling
interaction between concurrent processes. We define it via
\emph{simultaneous substitutions} \cite{Stoughton1988:SimultaneousSubst} \footnote{This choice
reflects the structure of our Rocq formalization more faithfully.}.

\begin{definition}[Substitution] \label{def:pi-fd:substitution}
  \begin{enumerate}
  \item A simultaneous substitution of messages $M_1, \hdots, M_n$ for
  the variables $x_1, \hdots, x_n$ ($x_i$ distinct) is defined by the
  following grammar.
  \[
    \sigma \Coloneqq [M_1, \hdots, M_n/x_1, \hdots, x_n]
  \]
  \item The domain $\dom(\sigma)$ and range $\rng(\sigma)$ of a substitution
  is defined as follows:
  \begin{align*}
    \dom([M_1, \hdots, M_n/x_1, \hdots, x_n]) &= \{x_1, \hdots, x_n\} \\
    \rng([M_1, \hdots, M_n/x_1, \hdots, x_n]) &= \fv(M_1) \cup \hdots \cup \fv(M_n)
  \end{align*}
  \item The action of a substitution $\sigma$ on processes and messages is defined
  recursively:
  {\allowdisplaybreaks
  \begin{align*}
    (\link{M_1}{M_2})\sigma &\coloneq \link{M_1\sigma}{M_2\sigma} \\
    (\parcomp{x}{y}{P}{Q})\sigma &\coloneq \parcomp{x'}{y'}{(P\sigma')\sigma}{(Q\sigma')\sigma} \\
    (P \seq xS)\sigma &\coloneq (P \sigma')\sigma \seq x'(S\sigma) \\
    \Lambda\sigma &\coloneq \Lambda \\
    x[M_1, \hdots, M_n/x_1, \hdots, x_n] &\coloneq M_i \quad \text{if } x = x_i \\
    x\sigma &\coloneq x \quad \text{if } x \notin \dom(\sigma) \\
    (\slselect{x.P})\sigma &\coloneq \slselect{x'.(P\sigma')\sigma} \\
    (\srselect{x.P})\sigma &\coloneq \srselect{x'.(P\sigma')\sigma} \\
    (\schoice{x.P}{y.Q})\sigma &\coloneq \schoice{x'.(P\sigma')\sigma}{y'.(Q\sigma')\sigma} \\
    (\ssend{x.P}{y.Q})\sigma &\coloneq \ssend{x'.(P\sigma')\sigma}{y'.(Q\sigma')\sigma} \\
    (\sreceive{x}{y}{P})\sigma &\coloneq \sreceive{x'}{y'}{(P\sigma')\sigma} \\
    (\sclose)\sigma &\coloneq \sclose \\
    (\swait{P})\sigma &\coloneq \swait{P\sigma}
  \end{align*}
  where $\sigma'$ is used to perform a renaming of bound variables with names
  that are fresh to both the domain and range of $\sigma$.
  }
  \end{enumerate}
\end{definition}

\begin{convention}[Barendregt Variable Convention]
  Throughout this thesis, we consider processes up to $\alpha$-equivalence.
  To simplify the presentation, we adopt the
  \emph{Barendregt variable convention}: in any definition, theorem, or proof, all bound names are
  assumed to be mutually distinct and disjoint from any free names occurring in the same context.
\end{convention}

\begin{definition}[Cons for substitutions] \label{def:pi-fd:subst-cons}
  Extending a substitution $\sigma = [M_1, \hdots, M_n/x_1, \hdots, x_n]$
  to additionally substitute $M$ for $x$, given $x \notin \dom(\sigma)$, is
  defined as
  \[
    (x \coloneq M) \cdot \sigma \coloneq [M, M_1, \hdots, M_n/x, x_1, \hdots, x_n]
  \]
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Identity substitution] \label{def:pi-fd:id-subst}
  Given a set of variables $\Gamma = \{x_1, \hdots, x_n\}$, we define
  the identity substitution as
  \[
    id_\Gamma \coloneq [x_1, \hdots, x_n/x_1, \hdots, x_n]
  \]
\end{definition}

The operational semantics itself only makes use of single substitution, which
we define in terms of the identity substitution.

\begin{definition}[Single substitution]
  We define a single substitution, written $P[M/x]$ ($M'[M/x]$),
  as the simultaneous identity substitution on free variables extended with a mapping for $x$:
  \[
    P[M/x] \coloneq (x \coloneq M) \cdot id_{\fv(P) \setminus x}
  \]
\end{definition}

Later in our development, we will also require a notion of \emph{well-formedness}
for substitutions.

\begin{definition}[Well-formedness of substitutions] \label{def:wf-substitution}
  Let $\sigma$ be a substitution with domain $\Gamma$ and range $\Delta$.
  The well-formedness of $\sigma$, written $\Gamma \vdash \sigma: \Delta$, is
  inductively defined by the following rules.
  \begin{center}
    \begin{prooftree}
      \hypo{}
      \infer1[\textsc{wf-subst-empty}]{\varnothing \vdash [] : \varnothing}
    \end{prooftree}

    \bigskip

    \begin{prooftree}
      \hypo{\Delta' \vdash M : A}
      \hypo{\Gamma \vdash \sigma : \Delta''}
      \hypo{\Delta = \Delta' \circ \Delta''}
      \infer3[\textsc{wf-subst-cons}]{x : A, \Gamma \vdash (x \coloneq M) \cdot \sigma : \Delta}
    \end{prooftree}

    \bigskip

    \begin{prooftree}
      \hypo{\Gamma \vdash \sigma : \Delta}
      \hypo{x \notin \dom(\Gamma)}
      \infer2[\textsc{wf-subst-cons-fv}]{\Gamma \vdash (x \coloneq M) \cdot \sigma : \Delta}
    \end{prooftree}
  \end{center}
\end{definition}

We later prove that well-formed substitutions preserve linear typing using the fact that
Definition \ref{def:wf-substitution} ensures that substituted messages are typed
in pairwise disjoint contexts.

\section{Operational Semantics} \label{ch:pi-fd:op-semantics}
We now define the operation of single-step \emph{contraction} $\contr$, which
allows to eliminate a single redex in a process.

\begin{definition}[Contraction $\contr$] \label{def:pi-fd:contr}
  \begin{align}
    % tensor/par
    \link{\ssend{v.P}{z.Q}}{\sreceive{a}{b}{R}}
    &\contr \parcomp{b}{z}{\parcomp{a}{v}{R}{P}}{Q}
    \tag{\textsc{r-$\otimes\parr$}}
    \\
    % plus/with
    \link{\slselect{z.P}}{\schoice{v.Q}{w.R}}
    &\contr \parcomp{z}{v}{P}{Q}
    \tag{\textsc{r-$\oplus\with_1$}}
    \\
    \link{\srselect{z.P}}{\schoice{v.Q}{w.R}}
    &\contr \parcomp{z}{w}{P}{R}
    \tag{\textsc{r-$\oplus\with_2$}}
    \\
    % 1/bot
    \link{\sclose}{\swait{P}}
    &\contr P
    \tag{\textsc{r-$1\bot$}}
    \\
    % AxCut
    \parcomp{x}{y}{\link{x}{M}}{P}
    &\contr P[M/y]
    \tag{\textsc{r-axcut}}
    \\
    % Seq
    P \seq xS
    &\contr P[S/x]
    \tag{\textsc{r-seq}}
  \end{align}
  \vspace{-2.0em}
  \begin{center}
    % congruence: cut
    \hfill
    \begin{prooftree}
      \hypo{P \contr P'}
      \infer1[]{\parcomp{x}{y}{P}{Q} \contr \parcomp{x}{y}{P'}{Q}}
    \end{prooftree}
    \hfill (\textsc{r-cong-cut})

    \bigskip

    % congruence: seq
    \hfill
    \begin{prooftree}
      \hypo{P \contr P'}
      \infer1[]{P \seq s \contr P' \seq s}
    \end{prooftree}
    \hfill
    (\textsc{r-cong-seq})

    \bigskip

    % struct
    \hfill
    \begin{prooftree}
      \hypo{P \equiv P'}
      \hypo{P' \contr Q'}
      \hypo{Q' \equiv Q}
      \infer3[]{P \contr Q}
    \end{prooftree}
    \hfill
    (\textsc{r-struct})
  \end{center}
\end{definition}

Rules \textsc{r-$\otimes\parr$}, \textsc{r-$\oplus\with_1$}, \textsc{r-$\oplus\with_2$},
and \textsc{r-$1\bot$} correspond to the logical cut reductions in Figure \ref{sec:linear-fd:logical-cuts}.
The rule \textsc{r-seq} eliminates a structural cut by permuting a rule
directly to its axiom. \textsc{r-axcut} incorporates the structural cut reductions \textsc{AxCut} and \textsc{LCut}
of $\LFD$ (Figure \ref{sec:linear-fd:structural-cut-reductions}), but in contrast to $\LFD$,
the resulting structural redex in \textsc{LCut} is immediately eliminated via substitution.

\textsc{r-cong-cut} and \textsc{r-cong-seq} allow contractions beneath cuts and
sequences. The former enables concurrent processes to be reduced in parallel;
the latter allows futures to be fulfilled in any order. However, $\contr$ is
not a congruence: Reduction within messages and statements is not possible.
Consequently, statements serve synchronization points in processes.

Finally, the rule \textsc{r-struct} allows to contract a redex in a different
process of same equivalence class w.r.t. $\equiv$. Sometimes,
$\equiv$ may reveal a redex that was not yet syntactically present in a process, e.g.
the process
\[
    \parcomp{z}{w}{\parcomp{x}{y}{P}{\link{z}{M}}}{Q}
\]
cannot apply \textsc{r-seq} using $\link{z}{M}$. However, assuming
$z \notin \fv(P)$ and $y \notin \fv(Q)$, the congruent process
\[
    \parcomp{x}{y}{P}{\parcomp{z}{w}{\link{z}{M}}{Q}}
\]
may contract to $\parcomp{x}{y}{P}{Q[M/w]}$.

Reduction $\red$ is now defined as a sequence of contractions up to congruence.

\begin{definition}[Reduction] \label{def:reduction}
  Reduction $\red$ is defined as the transitive closure of the union
  of contraction and structural congruence.
  \begin{equation*}
    \red \coloneq (\contr \cup \equiv)^*
  \end{equation*}
\end{definition}

\begin{remark}
  Instead of defining $\red$ in terms of a union, we could have defined
  $P \red Q$, if there exists $P_1, \hdots, P_n$, such that
  \[
    P = P_1 \contr P_2 \contr \hdots \contr P_{n-1} \equiv P_n = Q
  \]
  The resulting relations are the same, but many proofs benefit from Definition \ref{def:reduction}.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Axiom as Channel Creation}
The link process $\link{x}{y}$ has been introduced by Wadler \cite{Wadler2012:PropositionsAsSessions}.
It serves as a bidirectional link, forwarding anything received on $x$ to $y$
and vice versa. The same interpretation also holds in $\piFD$.

However, there is another way to interpret the forwarding process $\link{x}{y}$.
Since $\LFD$'s formula property extends to $\piFD$ as well,
any channel must first be created using $\textsc{t-ax}$. Thus, in its most primitive form,
$\textsc{t-ax}$ can be thought of as the command creating a new channel with endpoints named $x$ and $y$ adhering
to protocol $A$ and $A^\bot$, respectively,
\begin{center}
  \begin{prooftree}
    \hypo{}
    \infer1[\textsc{t-id}]{x : A \vdash x : A}
    \hypo{}
    \infer1[\textsc{t-id}]{y : A^\bot \vdash y : A^\bot}
    \infer2[\textsc{t-ax}]{x : A, y : A^\bot \vdash \link{x}{y}}
  \end{prooftree}
\end{center}
where $x$ and $y$ serve as memory cells remembering which side of a channel
has already been fulfilled.

This treatment of \textsc{t-ax} is different to other representations in the literature:
Bellin and Scott \cite{Bellin1994:PiCalcAndLinearLogic}, for example,
restrict the axiom to atomic types. This is dual to $\piFD$, where axioms
start at complex, composite types which are gradually eliminated.
Caires and Pfennig \cite{Caires2010:ILLasSessions}, on the other hand, have no axiom at all; the only
axiomatic rule is the proof of unit. Wadler's \cite{Wadler2012:PropositionsAsSessions}
axiom may be typed at arbitrary types, but $\CP$ also has an axiom for a proof of $1$.
As a consequence, in the calculi above, not every channel must go through an explicit creation via $\link{x}{y}$
as in $\piFD$.

\subsection{Cut as Parallel Composition}
Cut combines the constructs of parallel composition and scope restriction from
the $\pi$-calculus:

\begin{center}
  \begin{prooftree}
    \hypo{\Gamma, x : A \vdash P}
    \hypo{\Delta, y : A^\bot \vdash Q}
    \infer2[\textsc{t-cut}]{\Gamma \circ \Delta \vdash \parcomp{x}{y}{P}{Q}}
  \end{prooftree}
\end{center}

$P$ and $Q$ may execute in parallel and communicate via the channel endpoints
$x$ and $y$. Requiring every parallel composition to be enclosed by a scope
restriction is one of the insights of linear logic leading to deadlock freedom:
The typing ensures that $x$ and $y$ belong to different processes, whereas scope
restrictions in the untyped $\pi$-calculus may link endpoints belonging to the same thread.

The scope restriction allows $P$ to forward a message along $x$ which is received
by $Q$ along $y$. If either $P$ or $Q$ eventually reduces to a link which forwards
along $x$ or $y$, respectively, \textsc{r-axcut} implements a message passing between
the two concurrent processes.

As opposed to process interpretations based on classical linear logic, \textsc{cut} in
$\piFD$ only serves as a way to run communicating processes in parallel. Communication
that happens between endpoints of a channel is handled by links instead.

\subsection{Multiplicatives as Input and Output}
The multiplicatives $\otimes$ and $\parr$ model input and output: A channel
adhering to the session type $A \otimes B$ sends a message of type $A$ and then
behaves as $B$. Dually, a channel typed $A^\bot \parr B^\bot$ expects to receive
a message of type $A^\bot$ and continues as $B^\bot$.

If a process $P$ offers a channel $x : A \otimes B$, then $x$ can receive
a message of type $A \otimes B$:

\begin{center}
  \begin{prooftree}
    \hypo{\Gamma_1, x : A \otimes B \vdash P}
    \hypo{\Gamma_2, v : A^\bot \vdash Q}
    \hypo{\Gamma_3, z : B^\bot \vdash R}
    \infer2[\textsc{t-$\otimes$}]{\Gamma_2 \circ \Gamma_3 \vdash \ssend{v.Q}{z.R} : A \otimes B}
    \infer2[\textsc{t-seq}]{\Gamma_1 \circ \Gamma_2 \circ \Gamma_3 \vdash P \seq x\ssend{v.Q}{z.R}}
  \end{prooftree}
\end{center}

The message $\ssend{v.Q}{z.R}$ consists of two parts: A process $Q$, which computes
a message of type $A$ that will be forwarded along $v$ and a continuation process
$R$. The latter specifies a continuation channel $z$ which handles the
rest of the session protocol $B$.

As for \textsc{cut}, the typing guarantees that the output channel $v$ and the continuation channel
$z$ belong to two different processes. Therefore, the output cannot depend on its
own continuation, as may be the case in the untyped $\pi$-calculus, thus avoiding
deadlocks.

It is worth noting that $\piFD$ uses a new channel $z$ for $x$'s continuation.
Since every message in $\piFD$ also specifies a continuation
process, we can use an $\eta$-expanded link of the new continuation channel as a message's continuation
process to express asynchronous communication - the $\eta$-expansion now serving as a \emph{forwarder}:
Instead of sending the message $\ssend{v.Q}{z.R}$, we send the message
$M \coloneq \ssend{v.\link{v'}{v}}{z.\link{z'}{z}}$. Using \textsc{cut}, we can
now execute the output and continuation process before the message is received:
\[
  \parcomp{z'}{z}{\parcomp{v'}{v}{P \seq M}{Q}}{R}.
\]

This idea follows Boudol's \cite{Boudol1992:Asynchrony} translation from a synchronous $\pi$-calculus to an
asynchronous one. The relation of $\piFD$ to the
asynchronous $\pi$-calculus is further elaborated in Section \ref{sec:examples:async-communication}.

$x$'s dual endpoint, say $y$, is typed $A^\bot \parr B^\bot$:

\begin{center}
  \begin{prooftree}
    \hypo{\Gamma_1, y : A^\bot \parr B^\bot \vdash P}
    \hypo{\Gamma_2, a : A, b : B \vdash Z}
    \infer1[\textsc{t-$\parr$}]{\Gamma_2 \vdash \sreceive{a}{b}{Z} : A^\bot \parr B^\bot}
    \infer2[\textsc{t-seq}]{\Gamma_1 \circ \Gamma_2 \vdash P \seq y\sreceive{a}{b}{Z}}
  \end{prooftree}
\end{center}

The message $\sreceive{a}{b}{Z}$ specifies a process $Z$, which handles
an output of type $A$ along channel $a$ and the continuation along $b$.
If both $x$ and $y$ have received their messages, \textsc{r-$\otimes\parr$}
forks the receiving process $Z$, the output process $Q$ and the continuation process $R$
and links them accordingly:
\[
    \parcomp{b}{z}{\parcomp{a}{v}{Z}{Q}}{R}
\]

\subsection{Additives as Selection and Choice}
The additives $\oplus$ and $\with$ implement selection and binary choice.
A channel typed $A \oplus B$ may choose to continue the communication as either
$A$ or $B$, providing a corresponding continuation channel $z$ in a separate continuation process $Q$:

\begin{center}
  \begin{prooftree}
    \hypo{\Gamma_1, x : A \oplus B \vdash P}
    \hypo{\Gamma_2, z : A^\bot \vdash Q}
    \infer1[\textsc{t-$\oplus_1$}]{\Gamma_2 \vdash \slselect{z.Q} : A \oplus B}
    \infer2[\textsc{t-seq}]{\Gamma_1 \circ \Gamma_2 \vdash P \seq x\slselect{z.Q}}
  \end{prooftree}
\end{center}

The dual channel $y : A^\bot \with B^\bot$ offers a choice: A process
$R$ expecting to be forwarded a message of type $A$ along $a$ or, vice versa,
a process $Z$ waiting to communicate along $b$ according to the protocol $B$:

\begin{center}
  \begin{prooftree}
    \hypo{\Gamma_1, y : A^\bot \with B^\bot \vdash P}
    \hypo{\Gamma_2, a : A \vdash R}
    \hypo{\Gamma_2, b : B \vdash Z}
    \infer2[\textsc{t-$\with$}]{\Gamma_2 \vdash \schoice{a.R}{b.Z} : A^\bot \with B^\bot}
    \infer2[\textsc{t-seq}]{\Gamma_1 \circ \Gamma_2 \vdash P \seq x\schoice{a.R}{b.Z}}
  \end{prooftree}
\end{center}

\subsection{Units as Close and Wait}
A channel is closed using the multiplicative units $1$ and $\bot$.
The message sent along a channel $x : 1$ is an empty output:

\begin{center}
  \begin{prooftree}
    \hypo{\Gamma_1, x : 1 \vdash P}
    \hypo{}
    \infer1[\textsc{t-$1$}]{\vdash \sclose : 1}
    \infer2[\textsc{t-seq}]{\Gamma_1 \vdash P \seq x\sclose}
  \end{prooftree}
\end{center}

We interpret $\sclose$ as the message signaling channel termination.
A channel $y : \bot$, on the other hand, expects an empty input:

\begin{center}
  \begin{prooftree}
    \hypo{\Gamma_1, y : \bot \vdash P}
    \hypo{\Gamma_2 \vdash Q}
    \infer1[\textsc{t-$\bot$}]{\Gamma_2 \vdash \swait{Q} : \bot}
    \infer2[\textsc{t-seq}]{\Gamma_1 \circ \Gamma_2 \vdash P \seq y\swait{Q}}
  \end{prooftree}
\end{center}

Here, $\swait{Q}$ serves as a guard, which blocks the execution of $Q$.
Once $x$ signals a termination towards $y$, \textsc{r-$1\bot$} removes the guard
on $Q$ allowing it to be reduced.
