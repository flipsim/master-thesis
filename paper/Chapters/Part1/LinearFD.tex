%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\chapter{Linear Free Deduction}\label{ch:linear-fd}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

Parigot's Free Deduction was not presented in the context of linear logic.
The framework $\LFD$, on the other hand, can be regarded as a linear correspondent
to $\FD$.

\section{The Logical Framework LFD}
The propositions of $\LFD$ are the usual propositions
of linear logic, however, we omit the additive units.

\begin{definition}[Propositions]
  The propositions of $\LFD$ are inductively defined as follows
  \[
    A, B \Coloneqq X
              \mid X^\bot
              \mid A \otimes B
              \mid A \oplus B
              \mid A \parr B
              \mid A \with B
              \mid 1
              \mid \bot
  \]
\end{definition}

Opposed to Parigot's representation, we drop implication for ease of
representation and instead define linear implication $A \to B$ as usual ($A^\bot \parr B$).
Additionally, following other representations of classical linear logic, we no longer
use two-sided sequents. Instead, we collapse all formulas into the antecedent
via linear negation. Whether to collapse the formulas into the antecedent or
succedent plays little role in the logical framework, however, choosing
the former allows the connectives to retain their typical interpretation
in the process calculus setting.

\begin{definition}[Linear Negation]
  \begin{align*}
    (X)^\bot &\coloneq X^\bot
    & (X^\bot)^\bot &\coloneq X \\
    1^\bot &\coloneq \bot
    & \bot^\bot &\coloneq 1 \\
    (A \otimes B)^\bot &\coloneq A^\bot \parr B^\bot
    & (A \parr B)^\bot &\coloneq A^\bot \otimes B^\bot \\
    (A \oplus B)^\bot &\coloneq A^\bot \with B^\bot
    & (A \with B)^\bot &\coloneq A^\bot \oplus B^\bot
  \end{align*}
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[$\LFD$]
  \phantom{Foo} \\
  \begin{center}
    \begin{prooftree}
      \hypo{}
      \infer1[\textsc{ax}]{A, A^\bot \vdash}
    \end{prooftree}
    \\ \bigskip
    \begin{prooftree}
      \hypo{\Gamma_1, A \vdash}
      \hypo{\Gamma_2, A^\bot \vdash}
      \infer2[\textsc{cut}]{\Gamma_1, \Gamma_2 \vdash}
    \end{prooftree}
    \\ \bigskip
    \begin{prooftree}
      \hypo{\Gamma_1, A \oplus B \vdash}
      \hypo{\Gamma_2, A^\bot \vdash}
      \infer2[$\oplus_1$]{\Gamma_1, \Gamma_2 \vdash}
    \end{prooftree}
    \qquad
    \begin{prooftree}
      \hypo{\Gamma_1, A \oplus B \vdash}
      \hypo{\Gamma_2, B^\bot \vdash}
      \infer2[$\oplus_2$]{\Gamma_1, \Gamma_2 \vdash}
    \end{prooftree}
    \\ \bigskip
    \begin{prooftree}
      \hypo{\Gamma_1, A \with B \vdash}
      \hypo{\Gamma_2, A^\bot \vdash}
      \hypo{\Gamma_2, B^\bot \vdash}
      \infer3[$\with$]{\Gamma_1, \Gamma_2 \vdash}
    \end{prooftree}
    \\ \bigskip
    \begin{prooftree}
      \hypo{\Gamma_1, A \otimes B \vdash}
      \hypo{\Gamma_2, A^\bot \vdash}
      \hypo{\Gamma_3, B^\bot \vdash}
      \infer3[$\otimes$]{\Gamma_1, \Gamma_2, \Gamma_3 \vdash}
    \end{prooftree}
    \\ \bigskip
    \begin{prooftree}
      \hypo{\Gamma_1, A \parr B \vdash}
      \hypo{\Gamma_2, A^\bot, B^\bot \vdash}
      \infer2[$\parr$]{\Gamma_1, \Gamma_2 \vdash}
    \end{prooftree}
    \\ \bigskip
    \begin{prooftree}
      \hypo{\Gamma, 1 \vdash}
      \infer1[$1$]{\Gamma \vdash}
    \end{prooftree}
    \qquad
    \begin{prooftree}
      \hypo{\Gamma_1, \bot \vdash}
      \hypo{\Gamma_2 \vdash}
      \infer2[$\bot$]{\Gamma_1, \Gamma_2 \vdash}
    \end{prooftree}
  \end{center}
\end{definition}

Most notably, $\LFD$ incorporates \textsc{cut}, even though it remains derivable:

\begin{center}
  \begin{prooftree}
    \hypo{A \otimes 1, A^\bot \parr \bot \vdash}
    \hypo{\Gamma, A^\bot \vdash}
    \hypo{1, \bot \vdash}
    \infer1[$1$]{\bot \vdash}
    \infer3[$\otimes$]{\Gamma, A^\bot \parr \bot \vdash}
    \hypo{1, \bot \vdash}
    \hypo{\Delta, A \vdash}
    \infer2[$\bot$]{\Delta, A, 1 \vdash}
    \infer2[$\parr$]{\Gamma, \Delta \vdash}
  \end{prooftree}
\end{center}

\begin{remark}
  Negri proves a weaker result by showing that \textsc{cut} is admissible.
  The proof eliminates cuts by permuting them upwards towards their axioms
  where they can be trivially removed.
\end{remark}

A straightforward cut elimination procedure would thus replace
each occurrence of \textsc{cut} with the derivation above and eliminate logical and
structural redexes as in $\FD$.
However, we will not rely on the derivability of \textsc{cut} for normalization and
instead use a procedure that is closer to Negri's proof of admissibility.

The reason for this is that we will loosen the correspondence between cut elimination
in $\LFD$ and process reduction in $\piFD$. While cut elimination requires
contextual rewriting, i.e. we must eliminate cuts which lay high-up in the proof derivation,
processes of $\pi$-calculi are typically not allowed to be reduced in all contexts.
Instead, communication statements, which will later correspond to the logical elimination
rules, serve as synchronization points. As a consequence, the derivation of \textsc{cut}
above is not able to model two concurrent processes. This is why we treat redexes
involving \textsc{cut} differently.

The redexes of $\LFD$ are also divided into logical and structural ones.
The former are exactly the redexes in $\FD$, simply translated
into one-sided sequents (Figure \ref{sec:linear-fd:logical-cuts}).

\section{Proof Conversions}
Following \cite{Caires2010:ILLasSessions,Wadler2012:PropositionsAsSessions},
we consider cut elimination up to a class of
sound proof conversions (Figure \ref{sec:linear-fd:structural-proof-conversions}).
These proof conversions are not necessary for cut elimination,
but they avoid the need to specify symmetric versions for every computation rule (\textsc{CComm}, \textsc{LComm})
and allow cuts to be reordered (\textsc{CAssoc}), i.e. we do not have to start eliminating
the top-most cuts, but can also choose to eliminate lower cuts first by permuting them.
Together, these rules induce a congruence relation on proofs which corresponds
to the structural congruence of processes in the $\pi$-calculus.

\begin{figure}[tbh]
  \centering
  \rule{\textwidth}{1pt}
  \begin{equation*} \tag{\textsc{CComm}}
    \adjustbox{valign=t}{$\displaystyle
      \begin{prooftree}
        \hypo{\Gamma, A \vdash}
        \hypo{\Delta, A^\bot \vdash}
        \infer2[\textsc{cut}]{\Gamma, \Delta \vdash}
      \end{prooftree}
      \quad\equiv\quad
      \begin{prooftree}
        \hypo{\Delta, A^\bot \vdash}
        \hypo{\Gamma, A \vdash}
        \infer2[\textsc{cut}]{\Gamma, \Delta \vdash}
      \end{prooftree}
    $}
  \end{equation*}
  \begin{equation*} \tag{\textsc{CAssoc}}
    \adjustbox{valign=t}{$\displaystyle
      \begin{aligned}
        & \begin{prooftree}
            \hypo{\Gamma, A \vdash}
            \hypo{\Delta, A^\bot, B \vdash}
            \infer2[\textsc{cut}]{\Gamma, \Delta, B \vdash}
            \hypo{\Theta, B^\bot \vdash}
            \infer2[\textsc{cut}]{\Gamma, \Delta, \Theta \vdash}
          \end{prooftree} \\[1em]
        & \qquad\qquad \equiv\quad
        \begin{prooftree}
          \hypo{\Gamma, A \vdash}
          \hypo{\Delta, A^\bot, B \vdash}
          \hypo{\Theta, B^\bot \vdash}
          \infer2[\textsc{cut}]{\Delta, \Theta, A^\bot \vdash}
          \infer2[\textsc{cut}]{\Gamma, \Delta, \Theta \vdash}
        \end{prooftree}
      \end{aligned}
    $}
  \end{equation*}
  \begin{equation*} \tag{\textsc{LComm}}
    \adjustbox{valign=t}{$\displaystyle
      \begin{aligned}
        & \begin{prooftree}
            \hypo{A \odot B, A^\bot \overline{\odot} B^\bot \vdash}
            \hypo{P_1 \hdots P_n}
            \infer2[$\odot$]{\Gamma, A^\bot \overline{\odot} B^\bot \vdash}
            \hypo{P_1' \hdots P_k'}
            \infer2[$\overline{\odot}$]{\Gamma, \Delta \vdash}
          \end{prooftree} \\[1em]
        & \quad\equiv\quad
          \begin{prooftree}
            \hypo{A \odot B, A^\bot \overline{\odot} B^\bot \vdash}
            \hypo{P_1' \hdots P_k'}
            \infer2[$\overline{\odot}$]{\Delta, A \odot B \vdash}
            \hypo{P_1 \hdots P_n}
            \infer2[$\odot$]{\Gamma, \Delta \vdash}
          \end{prooftree}
      \end{aligned}
    $}
  \end{equation*}
  \rule{\textwidth}{1pt}
  \caption{Structural proof conversions}
  \label{sec:linear-fd:structural-proof-conversions}
\end{figure}

\section{Structural Cut Reductions}
Logical rules can be freely permuted upwards, just as in $\FD$. Thus,
the structural redexes of $\FD$ carry over to $\LFD$ and are eliminated in the same way.
However, the presence of \textsc{cut} adds new structural reductions. If we assume that
all logical rules are maximally permuted, then there are three new situations to consider.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item A cut where one of the derivation is an axiom. The reduction is given by the other derivation.
  \item A cut where one derivation is an axiom followed by an elimination rule, and
        the principal formula of the cut is the axiom's remaining formula. The reduction
        is given by removing the cut and applying the elimination rule to the other derivation.
  \item A cut where one derivation is an axiom followed by an elimination rule,
        but the cut's principal formula was not introduced by said axiom. In this
        case, the cut may be permuted into the elimination rule's premises.
\end{enumerate}

The first two reductions are instances of the new structural cut reductions depicted
in Figure \ref{sec:linear-fd:structural-cut-reductions}.
The latter refers to a class of conversions referred to
as \emph{commuting conversions}.
The following derivation illustrates a commuting conversion for $\otimes$.
\begin{equation*}
  \adjustbox{valign=t}{$\displaystyle
    \begin{aligned}
      & \begin{prooftree}
          \hypo{A \otimes B, A^\bot \parr B^\bot \vdash}
          \hypo{\Gamma_1, C, A^\bot \vdash}
          \hypo{\Gamma_2, B^\bot \vdash}
          \infer3[$\otimes$]{\Gamma_1, \Gamma_2, C, A^\bot \parr B^\bot \vdash}
          \hypo{\Delta, C^\bot \vdash}
          \infer2[Cut]{\Gamma_1, \Gamma_2, \Delta, A^\bot \parr B^\bot \vdash}
        \end{prooftree} \\[1em]
      & \quad \rhd\quad
      \begin{prooftree}
        \hypo{A \otimes B, A^\bot \parr B^\bot \vdash}
        \hypo{\Gamma_1, C, A^\bot \vdash}
        \hypo{\Delta, C^\bot \vdash}
        \infer2[Cut]{\Gamma_1, \Delta, A^\bot \vdash}
        \hypo{\Gamma_2, B^\bot \vdash}
        \infer3[$\otimes$]{\Gamma_1, \Gamma_2, \Delta, A^\bot \parr B^\bot \vdash}
      \end{prooftree}
    \end{aligned}
  $}
\end{equation*}

Even though these conversions are necessary for cut elimination, we will
omit them in our current development, following \cite{Caires2010:ILLasSessions}.
The reason for this is that they generally do not preserve process behavior,
because a formerly concurrent process of a cut is now blocked by a prefix
of another process.
Instead, they yield \emph{observational equivalences} according to
\emph{typed context bisimilarity} as defined by \cite{Perez2012:LinearLogicRelationsConcur}.
The insight is that if all formulas occurring in the context are cut
with appropriate derivations, the reordering cannot be detected once the
now blocking prefix performs its communication.

If we take commuting conversions into account, the normalforms of $\LFD$ are the
similar to the ones of $\FD$ except for use of one-sided sequents. However,
if we omit contextual rewriting and commuting conversions, each derivation of $\LFD$
has a \emph{weak normalform}.

\begin{definition}[Weak normalform]
  A derivation $\mathcal{D}$ is in weak normalform, if $\mathcal{D} \equiv \mathcal{N}$,
  where $\mathcal{N}$ is of the form
  \begin{center}
    \begin{prooftree}
      \hypo{\mathcal{D}_1}
      \hypo{\mathcal{D}_2}
      \hypo{\hdots}
      \hypo{\mathcal{D}_{n-1}}
      \ellipsis{}{\Gamma_{n-1}, A_{n-1}^\bot, C_{n-1} \vdash}
      \hypo{\mathcal{D}_n}
      \ellipsis{}{\Gamma_n, A_n^\bot, C_{n - 1}^\bot \vdash}
      \infer2[\textsc{cut}]{\Gamma_{n-1}', A_{n-1}^\bot, A_n^\bot, C_{n-2}^\bot \vdash}
      \infer1[]{\vdots}
      \infer3[\textsc{cut}]{\Gamma_2', A_2^\bot, \hdots, A_n^\bot, C_1^\bot \vdash}
      \infer2[\textsc{cut}]{\Gamma_1', A_1^\bot, A_2^\bot \hdots, A_n^\bot \vdash}
    \end{prooftree}
  \end{center}
  such that every $\mathcal{D}_i$ has the shape
  \begin{center}
    \begin{prooftree}
      \hypo{A_i, A_i^\bot \vdash}
      \hypo{(P_i)^j}
      \infer2[$\odot_i$]{\Gamma_i, A_i^\bot, C_i \vdash}
    \end{prooftree}
  \end{center}
  $\odot_i$ is the principal connective of the formula $A_i$ and $C_i$ is the $i$-th
  \textsc{cut}'s principal formula.
\end{definition}

A tighter computational correspondence thus typically involves either
modifying the $\pi$-calculus reduction strategy and its notion of structural congruence,
or adapting the cut elimination procedure.

Bellin and Scott worked out a tight correspondence between a synchronous $\pi$-calculus
and multiplicative linear logic \cite{Bellin1994:PiCalcAndLinearLogic}, which however
comes at the cost of additional congruences for processes allowing for two reads
to be permuted along the same thread. Caires and Pfenning \cite{Caires2010:ILLasSessions}, on the other hand,
achieve a tight correspondence only by restricting cut elimination to omit
commuting conversions. An exact correspondence has been developed by Honda
and Laurent \cite{Honda2010:PolarizedProofNets} for polarized linear logic and
the synchronous $\pi$-calculus with control \cite{Honda2004:PiCalculusWithControl}.
However, their cut elimination procedure is now no longer fully contextual and requires
that cuts are eliminated only if they are not enclosed by exponential boxes.
