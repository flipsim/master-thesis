\chapter{Free Deduction}\label{ch:free-deduction}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

In this section we introduce the logical Framework of \enquote{Free Deduction} (FD)
and discuss the normalization procedure for proofs of FD, as described by Parigot.
Using FD as a starting point, we derive the framework LFD in Section \ref{ch:linear-fd}.

\section{The Logical Framework FD}
For now, we will restrict ourselves to the connectives $\otimes$, $\oplus$, and $\to$.
Opposed to Parigot, we put an emphasize on the polarity (citation!) connectives.

\begin{definition}[Propositions of FD]
  The logical propositions of FD are defined inductively by the following grammar:
  \[
    A, B \Coloneqq X
              \mid A \oplus A
              \mid A \otimes A
              \mid A \to A
  \]
\end{definition}

Even though Parigot's representation of \enquote{Free Deduction} is not linear
and uses implicit structural rules, we still differentiate between multiplicative
and additive connectives as in linear logic. Through the course of this thesis,
we will restrict ourselves to the linear fragment of Free Deduction, leaving
the treatment of structural rules and exponentials to future work. As a consequence,
the logical rules of FD correspond to the linear core of Negri's UCL (citation!).

\begin{definition}[FD]
  \phantom{Foo} \\
  \begin{center}
    \begin{prooftree}
      \hypo{}
      \infer1[Ax]{A \vdash A}
    \end{prooftree}
    \\ \bigskip
    \begin{prooftree}
      \hypo{\Gamma_1, A \oplus B \vdash \Delta_1}
      \hypo{\Gamma_2 \vdash A, \Delta_2}
      \infer2[L$\oplus_1$]{\Gamma_1, \Gamma_2 \vdash \Delta_1, \Delta_2}
    \end{prooftree}
    \hfill
    \begin{prooftree}
      \hypo{\Gamma_1, A \oplus B \vdash \Delta_1}
      \hypo{\Gamma_2 \vdash B, \Delta_2}
      \infer2[L$\oplus_2$]{\Gamma_1, \Gamma_2 \vdash \Delta_1, \Delta_2}
    \end{prooftree}
    \\ \bigskip
    \begin{prooftree}
      \hypo{\Gamma_1 \vdash A \oplus B, \Delta_1}
      \hypo{\Gamma_2, A \vdash \Delta_2}
      \hypo{\Gamma_2, B \vdash \Delta_2}
      \infer3[R$\oplus$]{\Gamma_1, \Gamma_2 \vdash \Delta_1, \Delta_2}
    \end{prooftree}
    \\ \bigskip
    \begin{prooftree}[center=false]
      \hypo{\Gamma_1, A \otimes B \vdash \Delta_1}
      \hypo{\Gamma_2 \vdash A, \Delta_2}
      \infer[no rule]1{\Gamma_3 \vdash B, \Delta_3}
      \infer2[L$\otimes$]{\Gamma_1, \Gamma_2, \Gamma_3 \vdash \Delta_1, \Delta_2, \Delta_3}
    \end{prooftree}
    \hfill
    \begin{prooftree}[center=false]
      \hypo{\Gamma_1 \vdash A \otimes B, \Delta_1}
      \hypo{\Gamma_2, A, B \vdash \Delta_2}
      \infer2[R$\otimes$]{\Gamma_1, \Gamma_2 \vdash \Delta_1, \Delta_2}
    \end{prooftree}
    \\ \bigskip
    \begin{prooftree}[center=false, separation=1.0em]
      \hypo{\Gamma_1, A \to B \vdash \Delta_1}
      \hypo{\Gamma_2, A \vdash B, \Delta_2}
      \infer2[L$\to$]{\Gamma_1, \Gamma_2 \vdash \Delta_1, \Delta_2}
    \end{prooftree}
    \hfill
    \begin{prooftree}[center=false, separation=1.0em]
      \hypo{\Gamma_1 \vdash A \to B, \Delta_1}
      \hypo{\Gamma_2 \vdash A, \Delta_2}
      \infer[no rule]1{\Gamma_3, B \vdash \Delta_3}
      \infer2[R$\to$]{\Gamma_1, \Gamma_2, \Gamma_3 \vdash \Delta_1, \Delta_2, \Delta_3}
    \end{prooftree}
  \end{center}
\end{definition}

All sequents have the form $\Gamma \vdash \Delta$, where $\Gamma$ and $\Delta$
are unordered lists of formulas.

There are two observations that can be made about the structure of proofs
and provability in FD. First, opposed to classical sequent calculi like LK,
no logical rule of FD has an active formula in its conclusion. Every instance
of the logical rules removes the principal formula from the sequent entirely.
This causes proofs of FD to possess the \emph{formula property}.

\begin{definition}[Formula property]
  Every formula $A$ which occurs in the conclusion $\Gamma \vdash \Delta$ of a proof
  in FD comes from an axiom $A \vdash A$.
\end{definition}

\begin{remark}
  In a system with implicit structural rules, the property must be relaxed
  to every non-weakened formula $A$ and axiom of the shape $\Pi, A \vdash A, \Theta$.
  However, if one considers elimination rules for exponentials (see Negri'S UCL (citation!)),
  the formula property still holds in the presence of explicit structural rules.
\end{remark}

Consequently, a proof may only violate the subformula property if both formulas
of an axiom are eventually principal formula of an elimination rule, i.e.
if the proof contains both a left and right elimination with formulas of the same axiom
as subjects. This will later be used to define a notion of \emph{logical redex}.

Secondly, FD has no Cut rule. Surprisingly, the rule of Cut
\begin{center}
  \begin{prooftree}
    \hypo{\Gamma \vdash A, \Delta}
    \hypo{\Pi, A \vdash \Theta}
    \infer2[Cut]{\Gamma, \Pi \vdash \Delta, \Theta}
  \end{prooftree}
\end{center}
is derivable as can be witnessed by the following derivation:
\begin{center}
  \begin{prooftree}
    \hypo{A \to A \vdash A \to A}
    \hypo{A \vdash A}
    \infer2[L$\to$]{\vdash A \to A}
    \hypo{\Gamma, A \vdash \Delta}
    \hypo{\Pi \vdash A, \Theta}
    \infer3[R$\to$]{\Gamma, \Pi \vdash \Delta, \Theta}
  \end{prooftree}
\end{center}

The same construction can be carried out using the connective $\oplus$ or $\otimes$
if we also consider their units $0$ and $1$, and their corresponding elimination rules:

\begin{definition}[Units for FD]
  The meaning of the units $0$ and $1$ is defined by the following rules.
  \begin{center}
    \begin{tabular}{cc}
      \begin{prooftree}
        \hypo{\Gamma, 1 \vdash \Delta}
        \infer1[L$1$]{\Gamma \vdash \Delta}
      \end{prooftree}
      &
      \begin{prooftree}
        \hypo{\Gamma_1 \vdash 1, \Delta_1}
        \hypo{\Gamma_2 \vdash \Delta_2}
        \infer2[R$1$]{\Gamma_1, \Gamma_2 \vdash \Delta_1, \Delta_2}
      \end{prooftree}
      \\ \noalign{\bigskip}
      \text{no left elimination rule for $0$}
      &
      \begin{prooftree}
        \hypo{\Gamma \vdash 0, \Delta}
        \infer1[R$0$]{\Gamma \vdash \Delta}
      \end{prooftree}
    \end{tabular}
  \end{center}
\end{definition}

Cut can now also be derived using $\oplus$ and $0$ (and similarly, using $\otimes$ and $1$):

\begin{center}
  \begin{prooftree}
    \hypo{0 \oplus A \vdash 0 \oplus A}
    \hypo{\Gamma \vdash A, \Delta}
    \infer2[L$\oplus$]{\Gamma \vdash 0 \oplus A, \Delta}

    \hypo{0 \vdash 0}
    \infer1[R$0$]{0 \vdash}
    \hypo{\Pi, A \vdash \Theta}
    \infer3[R$\oplus$]{\Gamma, \Pi \vdash \Delta, \Theta}
  \end{prooftree}
\end{center}

The phenomenon that logical redexes derive cuts arises,
because cuts are \emph{internalized} in FD (as in natural deduction). However,
we can make them explicit by translating the derivations so that they only
use introduction rules and cuts, as described by \cite{Ostermann2022:IntroElim}:
\begin{center}
  \begin{prooftree}
    \hypo{0 \oplus A \vdash 0 \oplus A}
    \hypo{\Gamma \vdash A, \Delta}
    \infer1[R-$\oplus$-I$_2$]{\Gamma \vdash 0 \oplus A, \Delta}
    \infer2[Cut]{\Gamma \vdash 0 \oplus A, \Delta}
    \hypo{}
    \infer1[L-$0$-I]{0 \vdash}
    \hypo{\Pi, A \vdash \Theta}
    \infer2[L-$\oplus$-I]{\Pi, 0 \oplus A \vdash \Theta}
    \infer2[Cut]{\Gamma, \Pi \vdash \Delta, \Theta}
  \end{prooftree}
\end{center}
Removing the administrative redexes on the left and the resulting $\beta_\oplus$-redex
will leave us exactly with the rule of cut.

The translation demonstrates, that every elimination rule in FD can be derived
by its dual introduction rule and a cut in the
classical sequent calculus. Vice versa, every introduction rule
of the classical sequent calculus can be derived in FD by instantiating the main
premise with an axiom. This is possible because these rules are designed to mirror
each other: The side premises of FD's left (right) elimination rules are exactly
the premises of the dual right (left) introduction rules.

The duality between elimination  and introduction rules is a direct consequence
of the justifications for FD's logical rules. The introduction rules are justified
through the BHK-interpretation, which give the \emph{sufficient grounds} for
deriving a formula. Given the sufficient grounds, the elimination rules
can be justified through an \emph{inversion} and \emph{dual inversion principle} (cite Negri).
The former states
\begin{equation*}
  \parbox{0.85\textwidth}{\centering \itshape 
    ``Whatever follows from the grounds for deriving a formula must follow from that formula.''}
\end{equation*}
\vspace{-2ex}
\begin{flushright}
  --- \textsc{Negri}, \textit{Varieties in Linear Logic} (2001)
\end{flushright}

and determines the shape of the inference figure for which the principal formula
occurs in the succedent, i.e. the right-elimination form. For example,
the sufficient grounds for deriving the formula $A \otimes B$ is a proof
of $A$ and a proof $B$. From the inversion principle we obtain

\begin{center}
  \begin{prooftree}
    \hypo{\Gamma_1 \vdash A \otimes B, \Delta_1}
    \hypo{\Gamma_2, A, B \vdash \Delta_2}
    \infer2[]{\Gamma_1, \Gamma_2 \vdash \Delta_1, \Delta_2}
  \end{prooftree}
\end{center}

The dual inversion principle, conversely, determines the shape of the left-elimination form:
\begin{equation*}
  \parbox{0.85\textwidth}{\centering \itshape
    ``Whatever follows from a formula must follow from the sufficient grounds for deriving the formula.''}
\end{equation*}
\vspace{-2ex}
\begin{flushright}
  --- \textsc{Negri}, \textit{Varieties in Linear Logic} (2001)
\end{flushright}
This suggests the following inference figure.
\begin{center}
  \begin{prooftree}
    \hypo{\Gamma_1, A \otimes B \vdash \Delta_1}
    \hypo{\Gamma_2 \vdash A, \Delta_2}
    \hypo{\Gamma_3 \vdash B, \Delta_3}
    \infer3[]{\Gamma_1, \Gamma_2, \Gamma_3 \vdash \Delta_1, \Delta_2, \Delta_3}
  \end{prooftree}
\end{center}

It is these justifications that make Cut obsolete in FD. As such,
when considering a normalization procedure for FD, the notion
of \emph{cut-free} proofs has to be defined separately.
