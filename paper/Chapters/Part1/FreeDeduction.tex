%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\chapter{Free Deduction}\label{ch:free-deduction}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

In this section we introduce the logical Framework of \enquote{Free Deduction} ($\FD$)
and discuss the normalization procedure for proofs of $\FD$, as described by Parigot.
Using $\FD$ as a starting point, we derive the framework $\LFD$ in Section \ref{ch:linear-fd}.

\section{The Logical Framework FD}
For now, we will restrict ourselves to the connectives $\otimes$, $\oplus$, and $\to$.
Opposed to Parigot, we put an emphasis on
the polarity \cite{Andreoli1992:FocusingProofs, Zeilberger2008:UnityOfDuality} of connectives.

\begin{definition}[Propositions of $\FD$]
  The logical propositions of $\FD$ are defined inductively by the following grammar:
  \[
    A, B \Coloneqq X
              \mid A \oplus A
              \mid A \otimes A
              \mid A \to A
  \]
\end{definition}

Even though Parigot's representation of \enquote{Free Deduction} is not linear
and uses implicit structural rules, we still differentiate between multiplicative
and additive connectives as in linear logic. Through the course of this thesis,
we will restrict ourselves to the linear fragment of Free Deduction, leaving
the treatment of structural rules and exponentials to future work. As a consequence,
the logical rules of $\FD$ correspond to the linear core of Negri's $\UCL$ \cite{Negri2002:varieties-of-linear-calculi}.

\begin{definition}[$\FD$]
  \phantom{Foo} \\
  \begin{center}
    \begin{prooftree}
      \hypo{}
      \infer1[\textsc{ax}]{A \vdash A}
    \end{prooftree}
    \\ \bigskip
    \begin{prooftree}
      \hypo{\Gamma_1, A \oplus B \vdash \Delta_1}
      \hypo{\Gamma_2 \vdash A, \Delta_2}
      \infer2[\textsc{l$\oplus_1$}]{\Gamma_1, \Gamma_2 \vdash \Delta_1, \Delta_2}
    \end{prooftree}
    \hfill
    \begin{prooftree}
      \hypo{\Gamma_1, A \oplus B \vdash \Delta_1}
      \hypo{\Gamma_2 \vdash B, \Delta_2}
      \infer2[\textsc{l$\oplus_2$}]{\Gamma_1, \Gamma_2 \vdash \Delta_1, \Delta_2}
    \end{prooftree}
    \\ \bigskip
    \begin{prooftree}
      \hypo{\Gamma_1 \vdash A \oplus B, \Delta_1}
      \hypo{\Gamma_2, A \vdash \Delta_2}
      \hypo{\Gamma_2, B \vdash \Delta_2}
      \infer3[\textsc{r$\oplus$}]{\Gamma_1, \Gamma_2 \vdash \Delta_1, \Delta_2}
    \end{prooftree}
    \\ \bigskip
    \begin{prooftree}[center=false]
      \hypo{\Gamma_1, A \otimes B \vdash \Delta_1}
      \hypo{\Gamma_2 \vdash A, \Delta_2}
      \infer[no rule]1{\Gamma_3 \vdash B, \Delta_3}
      \infer2[\textsc{l$\otimes$}]{\Gamma_1, \Gamma_2, \Gamma_3 \vdash \Delta_1, \Delta_2, \Delta_3}
    \end{prooftree}
    \hfill
    \begin{prooftree}[center=false]
      \hypo{\Gamma_1 \vdash A \otimes B, \Delta_1}
      \hypo{\Gamma_2, A, B \vdash \Delta_2}
      \infer2[\textsc{r$\otimes$}]{\Gamma_1, \Gamma_2 \vdash \Delta_1, \Delta_2}
    \end{prooftree}
    \\ \bigskip
    \begin{prooftree}[center=false, separation=1.0em]
      \hypo{\Gamma_1, A \to B \vdash \Delta_1}
      \hypo{\Gamma_2, A \vdash B, \Delta_2}
      \infer2[\textsc{l$\to$}]{\Gamma_1, \Gamma_2 \vdash \Delta_1, \Delta_2}
    \end{prooftree}
    \hfill
    \begin{prooftree}[center=false, separation=1.0em]
      \hypo{\Gamma_1 \vdash A \to B, \Delta_1}
      \hypo{\Gamma_2 \vdash A, \Delta_2}
      \infer[no rule]1{\Gamma_3, B \vdash \Delta_3}
      \infer2[\textsc{r$\to$}]{\Gamma_1, \Gamma_2, \Gamma_3 \vdash \Delta_1, \Delta_2, \Delta_3}
    \end{prooftree}
  \end{center}
\end{definition}

All sequents have the form $\Gamma \vdash \Delta$, where $\Gamma$ and $\Delta$
are unordered lists of formulas.

There are two observations that can be made about the structure of proofs
and provability in $\FD$. First, opposed to classical sequent calculi like $\LK$,
no logical rule of $\FD$ has an active formula in its conclusion. Every instance
of the logical rules removes the principal formula from the sequent entirely.
This causes proofs of $\FD$ to possess the \emph{formula property}, which can
be verified by inspection of the rules of the calculus.

\begin{lemma}[Formula property]
  Every formula $A$ which occurs in the conclusion $\Gamma \vdash \Delta$ of a proof
  in $\FD$ comes from an axiom $A \vdash A$.
\end{lemma}

This is opposed to $\ND$, where the formula property only holds for the antecedent.
In $\LK$, the formula property holds for neither the antecedent nor the succedent.

\begin{remark}
  In a system with implicit structural rules, the property must be relaxed
  to every non-weakened formula $A$ and axiom of the shape $\Pi, A \vdash A, \Theta$.
  However, if one considers elimination rules for exponentials (see Negri's $\UCL$ \cite{Negri2002:varieties-of-linear-calculi}),
  the formula property still holds in the presence of explicit structural rules.
\end{remark}

Consequently, a proof may only violate the subformula property if both formulas
of an axiom are eventually principal formula of an elimination rule, i.e.
if the proof contains both a left and right elimination with formulas of the same axiom
as subjects. This will later be used to define a notion of \emph{logical redex}.

Secondly, $\FD$ has no \textsc{cut} rule. Surprisingly, the rule of \textsc{cut}
\begin{center}
  \begin{prooftree}
    \hypo{\Gamma \vdash A, \Delta}
    \hypo{\Pi, A \vdash \Theta}
    \infer2[\textsc{cut}]{\Gamma, \Pi \vdash \Delta, \Theta}
  \end{prooftree}
\end{center}
is derivable as can be witnessed by the following derivation:
\begin{center}
  \begin{prooftree}
    \hypo{A \to A \vdash A \to A}
    \hypo{A \vdash A}
    \infer2[\textsc{l$\to$}]{\vdash A \to A}
    \hypo{\Gamma, A \vdash \Delta}
    \hypo{\Pi \vdash A, \Theta}
    \infer3[\textsc{r$\to$}]{\Gamma, \Pi \vdash \Delta, \Theta}
  \end{prooftree}
\end{center}

The same construction can be carried out using the connective $\oplus$ or $\otimes$
if we also consider their units $0$ and $1$, and their corresponding elimination rules:

\begin{definition}[Units for $\FD$]
  The meaning of the units $0$ and $1$ is defined by the following rules.
  \begin{center}
    \begin{tabular}{cc}
      \begin{prooftree}
        \hypo{\Gamma, 1 \vdash \Delta}
        \infer1[\textsc{l$1$}]{\Gamma \vdash \Delta}
      \end{prooftree}
      &
      \begin{prooftree}
        \hypo{\Gamma_1 \vdash 1, \Delta_1}
        \hypo{\Gamma_2 \vdash \Delta_2}
        \infer2[\textsc{r$1$}]{\Gamma_1, \Gamma_2 \vdash \Delta_1, \Delta_2}
      \end{prooftree}
      \\ \noalign{\bigskip}
      \text{no left elimination rule for $0$}
      &
      \begin{prooftree}
        \hypo{\Gamma \vdash 0, \Delta}
        \infer1[\textsc{r$0$}]{\Gamma \vdash \Delta}
      \end{prooftree}
    \end{tabular}
  \end{center}
\end{definition}

\textsc{cut} can now also be derived using $\oplus$ and $0$ (and similarly, using $\otimes$ and $1$):

\begin{center}
  \begin{prooftree}
    \hypo{0 \oplus A \vdash 0 \oplus A}
    \hypo{\Gamma \vdash A, \Delta}
    \infer2[\textsc{l$\oplus$}]{\Gamma \vdash 0 \oplus A, \Delta}

    \hypo{0 \vdash 0}
    \infer1[\textsc{r$0$}]{0 \vdash}
    \hypo{\Pi, A \vdash \Theta}
    \infer3[\textsc{r$\oplus$}]{\Gamma, \Pi \vdash \Delta, \Theta}
  \end{prooftree}
\end{center}

The phenomenon that logical redexes derive cuts arises,
because cuts are \emph{internalized} in $\FD$ (as in natural deduction). However,
we can make them explicit by translating the derivations so that they only
use introduction rules and cuts, as described by \citet{Ostermann2022:IntroElim}:
\begin{center}
  \begin{prooftree}
    \hypo{0 \oplus A \vdash 0 \oplus A}
    \hypo{\Gamma \vdash A, \Delta}
    \infer1[\textsc{r-$\oplus$-i$_2$}]{\Gamma \vdash 0 \oplus A, \Delta}
    \infer2[\textsc{cut}]{\Gamma \vdash 0 \oplus A, \Delta}
    \hypo{}
    \infer1[\textsc{l-$0$-i}]{0 \vdash}
    \hypo{\Pi, A \vdash \Theta}
    \infer2[\textsc{l-$\oplus$-i}]{\Pi, 0 \oplus A \vdash \Theta}
    \infer2[\textsc{cut}]{\Gamma, \Pi \vdash \Delta, \Theta}
  \end{prooftree}
\end{center}
Removing the administrative redexes on the left and the resulting $\beta_\oplus$-redex
will leave us exactly with the rule of \textsc{cut}.

The translation demonstrates that every elimination rule in $\FD$ can be derived
by its dual introduction rule and a cut in the
classical sequent calculus. Vice versa, every introduction rule
of the classical sequent calculus can be derived in $\FD$ by instantiating the main
premise with an axiom. This is possible because these rules are designed to mirror
each other: The side premises of $\FD$'s left (right) elimination rules are exactly
the premises of the dual right (left) introduction rules.

The duality between elimination  and introduction rules is a direct consequence
of the justifications for $\FD$'s logical rules. The introduction rules are justified
through the BHK-interpretation, which give the \emph{sufficient grounds} for
deriving a formula. Given the sufficient grounds, the elimination rules
can be justified through an \emph{inversion} and \emph{dual inversion principle} \cite{Negri2002:varieties-of-linear-calculi}.
The former states

\begin{samepage}
\begin{equation*}
  \parbox{0.85\textwidth}{\centering \itshape 
    ``Whatever follows from the grounds for deriving a formula must follow from that formula.''}
\end{equation*}
\vspace{-3ex}
\nopagebreak
\begin{flushright}
  --- \textsc{Negri}, \textit{Varieties in Linear Logic} (2001)
\end{flushright}
\end{samepage}

and determines the shape of the inference figure for which the principal formula
occurs in the succedent, i.e. the right-elimination form. For example,
the sufficient grounds for deriving the formula $A \otimes B$ is a proof
of $A$ and a proof $B$. From the inversion principle we obtain

\begin{center}
  \begin{prooftree}
    \hypo{\Gamma_1 \vdash A \otimes B, \Delta_1}
    \hypo{\Gamma_2, A, B \vdash \Delta_2}
    \infer2[]{\Gamma_1, \Gamma_2 \vdash \Delta_1, \Delta_2}
  \end{prooftree}
\end{center}

The dual inversion principle, conversely, determines the shape of the left-elimination form:

\begin{samepage}
\begin{equation*}
  \parbox{0.85\textwidth}{\centering \itshape
    ``Whatever follows from a formula must follow from the sufficient grounds for deriving the formula.''}
\end{equation*}
\vspace{-3ex}
\begin{flushright}
  --- \textsc{Negri}, \textit{Varieties in Linear Logic} (2001)
\end{flushright}
\end{samepage}

This suggests the following inference figure.
\begin{center}
  \begin{prooftree}
    \hypo{\Gamma_1, A \otimes B \vdash \Delta_1}
    \hypo{\Gamma_2 \vdash A, \Delta_2}
    \hypo{\Gamma_3 \vdash B, \Delta_3}
    \infer3[]{\Gamma_1, \Gamma_2, \Gamma_3 \vdash \Delta_1, \Delta_2, \Delta_3}
  \end{prooftree}
\end{center}

It is these justifications that make \textsc{cut} obsolete in $\FD$. As such,
when considering a normalization procedure for $\FD$, the notion
of \emph{cut-free} proofs has to be defined separately.

\section{Cut Elimination}
As explained above, a detour in a proof of Free Deduction occurs when a proof
contains both a left and its corresponding right elimination.
In the simplest case, both rules are adjacent. However, this need not be the
case in general. Parigot thus differentiates between \emph{logical} and
\emph{structural cuts}.

\subsection{Logical Cuts}
A logical cut is of the form

\begin{center}
  \begin{prooftree}
    \hypo{}
    \infer1[\textsc{ax}]{A \vdash A}

    \hypo{\mathcal{D}_i}
    \ellipsis{}{\Pi_i \vdash \Theta_i}
    \delims{ \left( }{ \right)_{i} }
    \hypo{\hspace{-1.5em}} % by default, spacing between hypos is 1.5em

    \infer3[\textsc{l$\odot$}]{\Gamma_1 \vdash A, \Delta_1}

    \hypo{\mathcal{D}_k'}
    \ellipsis{}{\Pi'_k \vdash \Theta'_k}
    \delims{ \left( }{ \right)_{k} }
    \hypo{\hspace{-1.5em}}

    \infer3[\textsc{r$\odot$}]{\Gamma_1, \Gamma_2 \vdash \Delta_1, \Delta_2}
  \end{prooftree}
\end{center}
or
\begin{center}
  \begin{prooftree}
    \hypo{}
    \infer1[\textsc{ax}]{A \vdash A}

    \hypo{\mathcal{D}_i}
    \ellipsis{}{\Pi_i \vdash \Theta_i}
    \delims{ \left( }{ \right)_{i} }
    \hypo{\hspace{-1.5em}} % by default, spacing between hypos is 1.5em

    \infer3[\textsc{r$\odot$}]{\Gamma_1, A \vdash \Delta_1}

    \hypo{\mathcal{D}_k'}
    \ellipsis{}{\Pi'_k \vdash \Theta'_k}
    \delims{ \left( }{ \right)_{k} }
    \hypo{\hspace{-1.5em}}

    \infer3[\textsc{l$\odot$}]{\Gamma_1, \Gamma_2 \vdash \Delta_1, \Delta_2}
  \end{prooftree}
\end{center}

Both derivations refer to the same \emph{logical redex}, i.e. we will treat
them up to the order of their left and right elimination rule.
Their reduct is defined through a substitution
of minor premises of R$\odot$ (L$\odot$) for axioms in minor premises of
L$\odot$ (R$\odot$). The choice of which rule supplies the premises that are
being substituted for reflects the non-determinism of classical logic: Either
choice eventually normalizes the proof, but allowing both reductions comes
at the cost of losing confluence. For example, the logical redex for $\oplus$

\begin{center}
  \begin{prooftree}
    \hypo{}
    \infer1[\textsc{ax}]{A \oplus B \vdash A \oplus B}
    \hypo{\mathcal{D}_1}
    \ellipsis{}{\Gamma, A \vdash \Delta}
    \hypo{\mathcal{D}_2}
    \ellipsis{}{\Gamma, B \vdash \Delta}
    \infer3[\textsc{r$\oplus$}]{\Gamma, A \oplus B \vdash \Delta}
    \hypo{\mathcal{D}_3}
    \ellipsis{}{\Pi \vdash A, \Theta}
    \infer2[\textsc{l$\oplus_1$}]{\Gamma, \Pi \vdash \Delta, \Theta}
  \end{prooftree}
\end{center}
may reduce to
\begin{center}
  \begin{prooftree}
    \hypo{\mathcal{D}_1[\mathcal{D}_3 / A]}
    \ellipsis{}{\Gamma, \Pi \vdash \Delta, \Theta}
  \end{prooftree}
  \qquad or \qquad
  \begin{prooftree}
    \hypo{\mathcal{D}_3[\mathcal{D}_1 / A]}
    \ellipsis{}{\Gamma, \Pi \vdash \Delta, \Theta}
  \end{prooftree}
\end{center}

Parigot resolves this issue by statically removing any form of non-determinism.
For this purpose, he annotates every axiom with a sign which determines
the choice of substitution. This representation of Free Deduction avoids the
additional bureaucracy and reestablishes confluence by choosing
the substitution based on the polarity of the connective.

\begin{remark}
  Section \ref{ch:linear-fd} suggests yet another approach: Instead of
  relying on proof composition for normalization, we may reintroduce Cut
  and interpret $\FD$ proofs as proof nets.
\end{remark}

What remains is to discuss what we should do, if a left and right
elimination do not immediately succeed their axiom. Parigot refers to these
situations as \emph{structural redexes}.

\subsection{Structural Cuts}
Whenever a logical rule does not follow its axiom, we can make use of
the \emph{permutation property}, which states that every logical rule may freely
be permuted upwards towards its axiom. Generally speaking, if we want to permute
a logical rule above the conclusion of another logical rule, then we are looking
at a derivation of the following form:

\begin{center}
  \begin{prooftree}
    \hypo{P_1, \hdots, P_{i - 1}}
    \hypo{\mathcal{D}}
    \ellipsis{}{\Gamma_i, A \odot B \vdash \Delta_i}
    \hypo{P_{i + 1}, \hdots, P_n}
    \infer3[$\mathcal{R}$]{\Gamma, A \odot B \vdash \Delta}
    \hypo{P_1', \hdots, P_m'}
    \infer2[\textsc{l$\odot$}]{\Gamma' \vdash \Delta'}
  \end{prooftree}
\end{center}

$\mathcal{R}$ is either a left or right elimination rule, such that $A \odot B$
is not an active formula. Permuting $L\odot$ above $\mathcal{R}$ results in

\begin{center}
  \begin{prooftree}
    \hypo{P_1, \hdots, P_{i - 1}}

    \hypo{\mathcal{D}}
    \ellipsis{}{\Gamma_i, A \odot B \vdash \Delta_i}
    \hypo{P_1', \hdots, P_m'}
    \infer2[\textsc{l$\odot$}]{\Gamma'' \vdash \Delta''}

    \hypo{P_{i + 1}, \hdots, P_n}
    \infer3[$\mathcal{R}$]{\Gamma' \vdash \Delta'}
  \end{prooftree}
\end{center}

Here, the intermediate contexts $\Gamma''$ and $\Delta''$ are distinct from
$\Gamma'$ and $\Delta'$, as they are the result of applying \textsc{l$\odot$}
to the premise $\Gamma_i, A \odot B \vdash \Delta_i$---merging $\Gamma_i$ and $\Delta_i$
with the contexts from the side premises $P_1', \hdots, P_m'$.

\begin{remark}
  In a system with explicit structural rules, we would also have to consider
  the cases of permuting a logical rule above a weakening or contraction.
\end{remark}

Instead of permuting one rule at a time, we will permute a rule directly to its
axiom. This operation can be expressed through a substitution that replaces
the axiom $A \odot B \vdash A \odot B$ in $\mathcal{D}$ with the derivation

\begin{center}
  \begin{prooftree}
    \hypo{}
    \infer1[\textsc{ax}]{A \odot B \vdash A \odot B}
    \hypo{P_1', \hdots, P_m'}
    \infer2[\textsc{l$\odot$}]{\Pi \vdash A \odot B, \Theta}
  \end{prooftree}
\end{center}

Here, the contexts $\Pi$ and $\Theta$ represent the combined contexts
of $P_1', \hdots, P_m'$.

By continually permuting rules up to their axioms, we can identify logical redexes
and eliminate them. This procedure eventually terminates yielding a
cut-free proof which satisfies the subformula property. If a derivation contains
neither logical nor structural redexes, then it is in normal form.

\begin{definition}[Normal form]
  A derivation in $\FD$ is in normal form if every main premise is an axiom.
\end{definition}

The next step in our development involves some changes to $\FD$, that make
it more suited as the basis of a process calculus. The resulting
framework $\LFD$ will retain the notions of logical and structural cuts.
